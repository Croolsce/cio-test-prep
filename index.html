Lesson 1 · ~15 min

What Is Computational Intelligence Optimisation?

You've never attended a class. That's fine — here is the entire big picture, honestly explained.

Optimisation, in plain language

Optimisation = making something better: time, money, efficiency, fairness. You have many possible ways to do something (schedules, routes, allocations) and you want a good one — ideally the best, realistically an excellent one found in reasonable time.

Some problems are small enough to solve exactly with maths or brute force. But real problems — timetabling a university, routing vehicles, rostering staff — have astronomically many possible solutions. Checking them all would outlast the universe. Conventional exact methods choke. That's the gap this module fills.

The trick: copy nature

Computational Intelligence (CI) is a family of algorithms inspired by how nature solves hard problems without a mathematician in charge. Evolution breeds better animals; ant colonies find shortest paths; bird flocks converge on food. None of them enumerate every option — they search cleverly: try a population of candidate solutions, keep what works, vary it, repeat.

The CI family tree (from your Week-1 slides — the whiteboard mind-map):
  • Evolutionary Algorithms (EA) — mimic natural selection. The star of this module is the Genetic Algorithm (GA); siblings include Evolutionary Strategies, Differential Evolution, Genetic Programming.
  • Swarm Intelligence (SI) — mimic collective animal behaviour: PSO (birds), ACO (ants), ABC/BCO (bees), GWO (wolves).
  • Neural Networks and Fuzzy Systems — also CI branches, but not what this test is about.

Why does copying nature work? Because these mechanisms are general-purpose search strategies: they need no formula for the problem, only a way to score a candidate solution. That makes them ideal when the problem is messy, discrete, and full of constraints — exactly the problems in your test scenarios.

What this module actually assesses

Not maths proofs. Not coding (in the test). The skill is translation: take a messy real-world brief and translate it into a form a CI algorithm can search — then explain how the algorithm evaluates and improves candidate solutions. That translation is called encoding, and it's the heart of the test.

Mental model to keep: a CI algorithm is a blind cook. It cannot see the recipe book (the problem structure). It can only taste dishes (score solutions) and remix what tasted good. Your job in the test: design the dish format (encoding), the tasting rules (fitness), and describe an alternative cook (PSO/ACO).

Lesson 2 · ~20 min

Anatomy of an Optimisation Problem

Every scenario in every past paper decomposes into the same three ingredients. Learn to spot them and half of Q1 writes itself.

The three ingredients

IngredientQuestion it answersGrocery-run example (his Week-2 slide)
1. Decision variablesWhat are you actually choosing?Which items to buy, from which shop
2. Objective(s)How do you score a choice?Minimise cost, minimise trips
3. ConstraintsWhat must / must not hold?Budget limit, dietary needs, shop hours

Decision variables are the most important. In the test, every decision the scenario leaves open — which hall, which time slot, which staff member, whether a building is even opened — must eventually live inside your chromosome. The professor calls missing one “the commonest and most expensive error.”

Kinds of problems (vocabulary you'll reuse in answers)

  • Continuous vs discrete — choosing amounts (a temperature) vs choosing among options (which hall). Test scenarios are discrete, sometimes with continuous costs on top.
  • Single- vs multi-objective — one score vs several conflicting goals (cost and fairness and speed). Test scenarios are naturally multi-objective; you choose whether to combine goals into one score (weighted sum) or keep them separate (Pareto — Lesson 9).
  • Constrained vs unconstrained — test scenarios are heavily constrained; that's where marks hide.
  • Static vs dynamic — the test's problems are static (data doesn't change mid-solve), worth one line as an assumption.

Why these problems are genuinely hard

Try it — how fast search spaces explode

  • Combinatorial explosion — options multiply: n exams × k slots = kⁿ schedules. 20 slots and 40 exams ≈ 10⁵² schedules. Brute force is dead on arrival; this justifies a CI approach in your answer's opening line.
  • Local optima — “good enough” traps: a schedule that no single small change improves, yet far from the best. Nature-inspired search escapes via randomness (mutation) and populations exploring in parallel.
  • Interacting constraints — moving one exam to fix a clash creates two new ones. Improvements don't compose.

Reading a scenario like an examiner

Drill this ritual — it's the first 15 minutes of your test:

  1. Read once for the story. Read again with a pen, sorting every sentence into: entity data (things to schedule), resource data (halls, staff, machines), hard rule, soft preference, or cost.
  2. List the decisions. Ask: “what does the scenario leave up to me?” — assignment? order? timing? on/off activation?
  3. Write assumptions for anything the brief doesn't state (time granularity, resource behaviour). “I assume [X] because the brief does not state it.” Assumptions carry explicit marks.
His marking advice, from the mock

“The scenario doesn't mention holidays, so leave it out. You can add it, but it just makes your answer more complicated and therefore more things to go wrong.” Complexity you invent is risk you volunteered for.

Lesson 3 · ~20 min

The Genetic Algorithm, From Zero

The GA is the module's core algorithm and the assumed solver in Q1 and Q2. Here is the whole machine and its vocabulary — the words the examiner expects you to use.

The metaphor

Evolution improves species without a designer: individuals that fit their environment survive, breed, and pass on traits; random mutation adds novelty; over generations the population adapts. A GA does exactly this to solutions:

Biology wordIn a GA it meansPort example
IndividualOne complete candidate solutionOne full berth-and-crane schedule
ChromosomeThe encoded form of that solution — a string of values1231 = which section each ship uses
GeneOne slot in the chromosome — one decisionGene 3 = “ship 3 goes to section 3”
Allele / legal valuesThe values a gene may takeShip 2's gene may only be {2, 3}
PopulationThe set of individuals alive this generation (e.g. 100)100 different schedules
FitnessThe score saying how good an individual isWeighted cost of the schedule
GenerationOne full cycle of the loop below—
Genotype vs phenotypeThe encoded string vs the real-world solution it decodes toThe digits vs the actual timetable

The loop (memorise this — it opens many answers)

  1. Initialise a random population of chromosomes.
  2. Evaluate — decode each chromosome and compute its fitness.
  3. Select parents — better fitness ⇒ better chance of breeding (Lesson 6).
  4. Crossover — combine two parents' genes into offspring.
  5. Mutate — randomly tweak a few genes for novelty.
  6. Replace — form the next generation (usually keeping the best few unchanged: elitism).
  7. Repeat until a stopping condition: generation limit, time limit, or no improvement for k generations.
Watch a live GA solve a knapsack

10 items, capacity 400. Each bar = best fitness in the population that generation. This is the module's classic first example running for real, in your browser.

Exploration vs exploitation — the tension behind everything

Every design choice in a GA balances two forces:

  • Exploitation — concentrate on the best solutions found so far (strong selection pressure, elitism). Too much ⇒ premature convergence into a local optimum.
  • Exploration — keep trying new regions (mutation, diverse selection). Too much ⇒ random search that never settles.

Use these two words when justifying operators in Q1/Q3 — they are the professor's favourite axis (an entire lecture is titled around them).

Where the GA sits in your test answer

Q1 and Q2 assume a GA. You will never be asked to code one — you're asked to design its inputs: the chromosome (Lessons 4–5), the fitness function (Lessons 7–8), and to name sensible operators (Lesson 6). Q3 asks what changes if the cook is a swarm instead (Lessons 10–11).

Lesson 4 · ~25 min · core exam material

Encoding I — The Five Encodings

Encoding is the translation from real-world solution to chromosome. It is the single most examined skill in this module. Your Week-3 lecture calls these “the five encoding types you'll meet most often.”

The translation problem

A GA can only manipulate strings of values. So you must decide: how does a string of values represent a complete solution? That choice determines which operators work, which constraints can even be violated, and how big the search space is. Choose well and half your constraints disappear; choose badly and every offspring is invalid.

The five types

1 · Binary — yes/no decisions

One bit per decision. The classic: Knapsack — items with weight and value, a capacity; gene i = 1 means “take item i”. 01101

Also perfect for on/off activation decisions — e.g. “is this building opened for this session?” (that exact decision was in the April 2026 paper).

Try it — knapsack encoding, live fitness

Click items to toggle their gene. Capacity 400. Watch validity and fitness respond.

2 · Integer — picking from a list

Each gene holds an index into a list of options. The workhorse of scheduling: gene per exam = hall index; gene per ship = section number; gene per guard-shift = 0/1/2. SH11SH22SH33SH41

Killer move (worth marks): restrict each gene's legal values to what that entity is allowed. If ship 2 only fits sections {2,3}, its gene simply cannot hold 1 — the constraint becomes unwritable and the search space shrinks (in the walkthrough: 81 → 36).

3 · Real-valued — continuous numbers

Genes are floats. Used for tuning continuous parameters (e.g. GA-tuned hyperparameters in your lab) — and crucially for random keys, the trick that lets PSO handle discrete problems (Lesson 10).

4 · Permutation — when ORDER matters

The chromosome is an ordering; every ID appears exactly once. The classic: Travelling Salesperson (TSP) — visit order of cities. 3142 = visit city 3, then 1, then 4, then 2.

Also: job service order, priority order for a greedy placer. Warning you must write in the exam: standard crossover breaks permutations (you'd get a city twice). Permutations need order-preserving operators — OX / PMX crossover, swap or inversion mutation (Lesson 6).

5 · Grid / matrix (2-D)

Rows = resources, columns = time slots, cell = what's happening there. A timetable drawn literally. Exclusivity is automatic (one entry per cell) — but offspring from crossover are almost never valid, so it's repair-heavy. Usually the design you mention and reject, which itself earns justification marks.

Properties of a good encoding (Week-3 slide, near-verbatim)

  • Completeness — every real solution can be represented.
  • Validity bias — ideally every chromosome decodes to a legal solution (or invalidity is rare/cheap to fix).
  • Non-redundancy — one solution ↔ ideally one chromosome; redundant encodings waste search.
  • Locality — small gene change ⇒ small solution change; operators then improve rather than scramble.
  • Operator compatibility — standard crossover/mutation produce sensible children.

Exam habit: after proposing your encoding, spend two sentences scoring it against these properties. That's the “justification” 2 marks nearly every year.

Lesson 5 · ~30 min · core exam material

Encoding II — Real Scenarios & the Four Patterns

Test scenarios need several decisions encoded at once: assignment AND order AND time. This lesson gives you the four reusable chromosome architectures from the professor's walkthrough — one of them will fit whatever scenario Friday brings.

Heterogeneous vs homogeneous (his whiteboard vocabulary)

Heterogeneous

The chromosome has segments of different gene types — e.g. segment A: integer section per ship; segment B: permutation of jobs. Each segment gets its own mutation/crossover.

1231‖2413

Simple genes, more of them; light decoder.

Homogeneous + decoder

One uniform gene type (e.g. just a sequence of session IDs) and a smarter decoder that allocates day / slot / room to each as it reads. The whiteboard line: “the decoder is responsible for allocating day, slot, classroom to each session.”

Simpler chromosome ⇄ heavier decoder. Naming this trade-off = marks.

The four patterns (from the walkthrough deck — learn all, deploy one)

Pattern 1 · Assignment vector + permutation — the workhorse

Segment A: one integer gene per entity = its resource/group. Segment B: a permutation = service order. The decoder derives all timing.

Strengths: short; standard operators per segment (uniform crossover on A, OX on B); compatibility enforced in the alphabet so offspring are always legal. Weakness: the segments interact — a good order for one assignment can be bad after crossover swaps the assignment.

Default choice. If unsure on Friday, use this.

Pattern 2 · Block / grouping chromosome — the elegant one

One sequence of entity IDs cut by separator genes. Which block an entity sits in IS its assignment; its position in the block IS its order.

SH1SH4‖SH2‖SH3 → “Section A serves SH1 then SH4; B serves SH2; C serves SH3.”

Strengths: shortest possible; assignment and order can never contradict; workload visible per block; maps to the recognised grouping GA family (name it — it reads as expertise). Weakness: standard crossover shreds it — needs a block-aware crossover or a repair step. Admit this cost explicitly.

Pattern 3 · Direct heterogeneous segments, no decoder — the cautionary tale

One gene per entity per decision (assignment, resource, start slot); the chromosome IS the schedule, read directly.

Strength: no decoder to explain. Weakness: offspring almost never valid — two things collide constantly, so you drown in penalties/repair. In the walkthrough its verdict is “it goes wrong a lot.” Use it as your rejected alternative for the justification marks.

Pattern 4 · Resource × time grid

Rows = resources, columns = slots, each cell holds the entity (and secondary resource) occupying it. Exclusivity becomes structurally impossible to violate — but crossover of two grids almost never yields a valid child, so everything needs repair. Mention when the scenario is literally a timetable; usually rejected too.

The decoder — always describe it in numbered phases

The decoder turns genotype into phenotype. For scheduling scenarios it is nearly always two-phase, and saying why the order is forced is worth marks:

  1. Phase 1 — build the allocation plan. Read assignment genes → group entities per resource → order each group (by permutation segment, or by ETA/priority rule) → lay out time windows respecting release times and changeover buffers.
  2. Phase 2 — layer the secondary schedule. Assign cranes / invigilators / rooms greedily: for each placed entity take the fastest/cheapest legal resource, inserting breaks, charging, rest periods as the rules demand.

The sentence that earns the mark: “The decoder must build the berth plan before the crane schedule, because crane availability cannot be evaluated until ship positions and times are known.” — Adapt to any scenario.

Worked micro-example — guard rota (the whiteboard scenario)

16 guards, 3 shifts/day, 7 days. Decisions: which guard works which shift on which day.

  • Binary flattened 3-D: gene (g, d, s) = 1 if guard g works shift s on day d. Length 16×7×3 = 336 bits. Heterogeneous reading order must be stated (“the first 21 genes are guard A's week…”).
  • Homogeneous alternative: a sequence of shift-slot IDs with a decoder assigning guards respecting rules. Both appear on his whiteboard — comparing them is the justification.

Lesson 6 · ~25 min

The GA Engine — Selection, Crossover, Mutation

A 48-slide lecture (“Deconstructing a Genetic Algorithm”) lives in this module just for these operators. In the test they appear inside Q1 justifications and Q3 comparisons.

Selection — who gets to breed

MethodHow it worksCharacter
Roulette wheelEach individual gets a wheel slice proportional to fitness; spin once per parentSimple; breaks down when one fitness dominates or fitnesses are negative
Stochastic Universal SamplingOne spin, N evenly spaced pointersRoulette with less luck variance
Rank-basedSlice by rank position, not raw fitnessKeeps pressure steady even with extreme fitness gaps

Try it — spin a roulette wheel

Four schedules with fitness 40 / 30 / 20 / 10 → slice sizes 40% / 30% / 20% / 10%. Spin repeatedly: good solutions are favoured, not guaranteed. That's selection pressure with exploration preserved.

Winners so far: —

Crossover — recombining parents

  • One-point: cut both parents at one position, swap tails. 10|11
  • Two-point / n-point: swap the middle segment(s).
  • Uniform: each gene tosses a coin for which parent it comes from — highest mixing.
  • For permutations — Order Crossover (OX): copy a slice from parent 1, fill remaining positions in parent 2's order, skipping duplicates. PMX (partially mapped) is the named alternative. These preserve the every-ID-exactly-once property that standard crossover destroys.
Try it — Order Crossover (OX), step by step

Two parent tours of 6 cities. Press Step.

Mutation — the novelty tap

EncodingMutation
BinaryBit-flip with small probability per gene
IntegerRandom-reset within that gene's legal values
RealGaussian nudge
PermutationSwap two positions, or invert a slice

Low mutation ⇒ stagnation in local optima; high ⇒ random search. Typical rates: crossover probability ~0.7–0.9, mutation per-gene ~1/length.

Elitism & parameters

Elitism copies the best 1–2 individuals unchanged into the next generation — it prevents “bad-luck loss” of the best solution and is the E in NSGA-II's elitist design (Lesson 9). Parameters you can name when asked what you'd tune: population size, generations, crossover rate, mutation rate, tournament size, elite count.

Exam link: after choosing an encoding in Q1, one sentence on operators: “Uniform crossover and legal-value reset mutation on the assignment segment; OX and swap mutation on the permutation segment; tournament selection (k=3) with elitism of 2.” Precision like this reads as mastery and costs you 20 seconds.

Lesson 7 · ~20 min · core exam material

Hard & Soft Constraints — and the Five Tools

Q2 opens with validity. The skill: sort every rule in the scenario into hard vs soft, then pick the cheapest tool to enforce each. This exact framework is in the walkthrough's “sort the rules” and “pick the cheapest tool” slides.

Hard vs soft

Hard constraints — validity

Physically or legally impossible to break. A solution violating one is invalid, not just bad.

  • Capacity exceeded
  • Two uses of one resource at once (exclusivity)
  • Start before release time / ETA
  • Mandatory gaps: changeover, cleaning buffer, 48-h chain gap
  • Compatibility / certification (GPU lab, hazmat berth)
  • Staffing minimums, pairing rules (junior + senior)
  • Working-time limits

Soft constraints — quality

Preferences and costs. Violating them makes a solution worse, not illegal — they become fitness components (Lesson 8).

  • Waiting time, delays
  • Money / energy cost
  • Fairness / workload balance
  • Serving priorities early
  • Congestion, student comfort
  • Preferences (guard shift wishes)

Write validity as numbered checkable tests, each with a concrete example from your own Q1 timetable: “Check 3 — changeover: ≥30 min between one ship leaving a berth and the next arriving. In my worked example: out 10:30, in 11:00 ✓.” Enumerating them explicitly is 3 marks.

The five tools, cheapest first

#ToolHowBest for
1Encoding (alphabet restriction)Restrict legal gene values so the violation cannot be writtenCompatibility / eligibility rules. Bonus: shrinks the search space
2Decoder (construction)The decoder lays solutions out so the rule never arises — sequential placement means no overlaps; buffers auto-insertedExclusivity, changeover, release times
3RepairDetect the violation in an offspring and fix it (swap to nearest legal option)When crossover breaks structure (blocks, permutations)
4Penaltyfitness += weight × amount of violationWhatever remains; when degree of violation matters
5RejectionDiscard invalid offspring, generate againLast resort — wasteful. Mention it only to dismiss it

Present this as a mini-table in your Q2 answer — one row per constraint: constraint → tool → one-line how. The table format itself matches how the marks are awarded (3 for “constraint handling is reasoned”).

Penalty design — the lab formula

Your guard-rota lab (Guard.py) uses the canonical shape — memorise it:

fitness = (hardViolation₁ + hardViolation₂ + hardViolation₃) × BIG_WEIGHT + softPreferenceViolations

  • Hard violations share one large weight so any hard break outweighs every soft gain — the GA is steered to feasibility first, quality second.
  • Count the degree of violation (how many, how far over), not just yes/no — gives the GA a gradient to climb down.
  • Alternative sentence: “rewards for meeting criteria or penalties for violating them” — his phrasing from the knapsack session.
Try it — penalty fitness, live

A guard rota with sliders for violations. Watch feasibility dominate.

Lesson 8 · ~25 min · core exam material

Designing the Fitness Function

The heart of Q2: turn every soft goal into a component with a formula, a unit, and a purpose — then combine them honestly.

The component recipe

For each thing the scenario says it cares about, write one component: fᵢ = formula (unit) — purpose. The standard library, adaptable to any scenario:

ComponentFormula shapeUnit
f₁ waiting / delayΣ over entities (service start − release time)minutes
f₂ priority-weighted delayΣ wₑ × delayₑ, with w = 5 emergency / 3 high / 1 normalweighted min
f₃ resource utilisation1 − (hours worked ÷ hours available)fraction
f₄ overload / congestionΣ over resources & slots max(0, occupancy − capacity)unit-hours
f₅ money / energy costΣ usage-hrs × rate + activation feesRM
f₆ overtime / limit breachesΣ hours beyond allowedhours
f₇ allocation costΣ cost(entity, its assigned resource) from the cost matrixRM
f₈ balance / fairnessstandard deviation of load across resources or staffmixed
f₉ makespanfinish time of the last jobminutes

Include the components the scenario's special features create — building activation fees, time-window traffic penalties, seating buffers. Those bespoke components are where the 4 “components explained” marks concentrate.

Normalise before you combine

You cannot add minutes to ringgit to fractions. Scale every component to [0,1] first:

f′ᵢ = (fᵢ − fᵢ,min) ÷ (fᵢ,max − fᵢ,min)

Justify each min/max from the scenario's own worst case (“max waiting = every ship waits the whole shift = 600 min”). Then a weighted sum, weights summing to 1, lower = better: F = Σ wᵢ·f′ᵢ. If the scenario says “X is twice as important as Y” — set wₓ = 2wᵧ and cite the line.

The makespan story — his most important slide

“The most important slide” (walkthrough, verbatim structure)

Two fully valid schedules. Intuition says X is better — the quay is clear by 14:00 instead of 15:30. But the weighted sum scores Y better. Why? Nothing in f₁…f₈ measured when the quay is clear. Waiting time only measures the start of service. The fix: add makespan f₉, re-spread the weights — the ranking flips.

The generalised lesson to write in your answer: “If the ranking disagrees with intuition, I ask what the intuition is measuring that no component captures — then add that component.”

Try it — watch the ranking flip

Schedules X and Y from the walkthrough. Drag the makespan weight from 0 upward (other weights re-scale proportionally) and watch the winner change.

Critical awareness — the 3 free marks

  • Objectives conflict: cheap berths attract every ship ⇒ congestion; low cost fights low waiting. Name one concrete conflict from the scenario.
  • Weights are policy, not fact: “the ranking depends entirely on which components I chose and how I weighted them — neither is a fact about the port.”
  • Time-dependent terms (a 12:00–14:00 traffic penalty) must be computed per assigned slot, not as a constant.

Lesson 9 · ~20 min

Multi-Objective Optimisation & Pareto

The alternative to weighted sums: refuse to choose. Two full lectures cover this (Multi-Objective Foundations, NSGA-II) — in the test it's your strongest Q2 aggregation argument and a legitimate Q3 pick.

Dominance — the core idea

With two goals (say minimise cost, minimise waiting), when is schedule A simply better than B? When A is at least as good on every objective and strictly better on at least one. Then A dominates B. If each wins on a different objective, neither dominates — they're both defensible, just different trade-offs.

Try it — click the Pareto front

Each dot is a schedule (lower-left is better on both axes). Click every dot you think is non-dominated, then check.

The set of all non-dominated solutions is the Pareto-optimal set; plotted, it forms the Pareto front — the menu of best-available trade-offs. The decision-maker (port manager, registrar) picks from the menu using judgement the algorithm doesn't have.

NSGA-II in one paragraph (his “in one sentence” slide, expanded)

NSGA-II = Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II — a GA whose selection is driven by Pareto rank instead of one score. Each generation: create offspring Q from parents P, combine R = P ∪ Q (this is elitism — parents compete with children), sort R into fronts (rank 1 = non-dominated; remove them, rank 2 = non-dominated among the rest, …), fill the next population front by front. When a front only half-fits, break the tie by crowding distance — prefer solutions in sparse regions of the front, so the menu stays spread out instead of clustering (his hospital-manager example: a clustered front gives the manager no real choice).

When to argue which (Q2's aggregation marks)

Weighted sum

One number, one answer, quick. Use when: the organisation has a stated policy you can cite; one schedule is needed now; limited writing time. Danger (say it): the ranking is an artefact of your weights.

Pareto / NSGA-II

Keep objectives separate; return a front of non-dominated schedules; the manager picks. Use when objectives genuinely conflict and no defensible weights exist. Cost: more complex, needs a human decision at the end.

Exam move: propose the weighted sum as your main answer (it's faster to write and matches the mark scheme), then add 3 sentences: “Alternatively, keeping objectives separate and using NSGA-II would return a Pareto front of trade-off schedules… I would prefer this when no defensible weighting exists.” That paragraph covers aggregation + critical awareness at once.

Lesson 10 · ~20 min · Q3 material

Particle Swarm Optimisation

Q3's most popular answer: the cheapest swap away from a GA. From the swarm lecture: “roughly a third of your test marks relate to this family — explain it in words, applied to a scenario; no code.”

The metaphor

A bird flock finds food none of them has seen: each bird follows a blend of its own best experience and the flock's best find. The Week-slides list the three flocking principles — collision avoidance, directional matching (velocity matching), flock centring.

The mechanics (know these names)

  • Each particle is a position x in the search space — a vector of real numbers — plus a velocity v.
  • It remembers its personal best position pbest; the swarm shares the global best gbest.
  • Every step: v ← ω·v + c₁r₁(pbest − x) + c₂r₂(gbest − x), then x ← x + v.
  • ω = inertia (exploration), c₁ = cognitive pull (own memory), c₂ = social pull (swarm's find), r₁ r₂ = random numbers. That equation in words: “keep some momentum, drift toward what worked for me, drift toward what worked for anyone.”
Watch a swarm converge

Particles seek the greenest point of a hidden fitness landscape. Watch exploration collapse into exploitation around gbest.

The exam problem — PSO is continuous, your scenario is discrete

Ships and exams aren't real numbers. The bridge (and the walkthrough's chosen Q3 answer) is random keys:

  • Assignment key: one real in [0,1] per entity; split [0,1] into bands over that entity's legal resources only. If ship 3 may use only sections {A, C}: 0–0.5 → A, 0.5–1 → C. Compatibility survives the move to PSO — say this explicitly.
  • Order key: one real per entity; within a group, sort ascending — smallest key is served first (“Smallest Position Value” decoding).

So a particle = 2 real numbers per entity, and your existing decoder and fitness function run unchanged on the decoded result. “That is the point worth making” — the walkthrough's own words.

Q3 paragraph, ready to adapt

Encoding/decoding: instead of a discrete chromosome, each particle is a vector of random keys — one banded assignment key per entity (bands restricted to legal resources, preserving compatibility) and one order key (sorted ascending within each group). My two-phase decoder is reused unchanged. Evaluation: the fitness function carries over completely unchanged. Implementation: no crossover or mutation operators to design; parameters are inertia ω, cognitive c₁, social c₂, and swarm size; less code than the GA. Risks: many key vectors decode to the same schedule (redundancy) and premature convergence — mitigated by inertia decay or velocity clamping. Judgement: I would prefer PSO when implementation effort is the constraint and my decoder already guarantees feasibility.

Lesson 11 · ~20 min · Q3 material

Ant Colony Optimisation (and the Bees)

The Q3 answer that scores when constraints are dense: ACO never creates an invalid solution, because it never offers an illegal move.

The metaphor

Ants find shortest paths with no map: each ant lays pheromone on its route; shorter routes get re-walked sooner, accumulating pheromone faster; ants probabilistically prefer stronger trails; evaporation fades unused ones. Indirect coordination through the environment — stigmergy.

The mechanics

  • No chromosome, no crossover. Each ant constructs a complete solution one decision at a time.
  • At each step, choose among currently legal options with probability ∝ τᵅ · ηᵝ — pheromone τ (learned desirability) × heuristic η (greedy hint, e.g. 1/distance or 1/cost), balanced by exponents α and β.
  • After all ants finish: evaporate (τ ← (1−ρ)τ) then deposit — better solutions add more pheromone to the decisions they used. Elitist / rank-based variants let only the top ants deposit (that was literally your lab exercise, plus a “depreciation factor” task).
Watch ants find the short path

Ants choose between routes on a small graph. Line thickness = pheromone. Watch the short path win as evaporation starves the long one.

Applying ACO to a scheduling scenario (the Q3 skill)

One ant builds one schedule: take the next unplaced entity (earliest release / highest priority first) → list only resources that are legal right now (compatible, free, within limits) → pick weighted by τ×η → repeat until complete. Hard constraints are handled by construction: compatibility, occupancy, changeover can never be violated because illegal moves are never on the menu. No penalties, no repair.

Encoding/decoding: there is no chromosome to design — construction replaces the decoder; pheromone lives on (decision → option) pairs, e.g. τ(ship, section). Evaluation: the fitness function is unchanged, but it now also drives pheromone deposit (better schedule ⇒ more pheromone on its choices). Implementation: parameters α, β, evaporation ρ, number of ants; needs a pheromone matrix and per-step legality checks — more bookkeeping than PSO, but constraint handling comes free. Judgement: I would prefer ACO when hard constraints are dense and interlocking, because feasibility is guaranteed by construction rather than penalties or repair.

Bees, briefly (breadth insurance)

BCO / Bees Algorithm: scout bees explore randomly; the best sites recruit more foragers via the waggle dance (exploitation) while other scouts keep searching (exploration). Your lab solved TSP with it. One sentence in Q3 shows breadth; don't spend more.

Grouping GA + local search (third Q3 option)

If your Q1 used the block chromosome: keep it identical (“nothing changes — that is the argument for it”), swap in block crossover (copy whole blocks parent→child, delete duplicates, reinsert missing entities at cheapest legal position) and add local search (swap two entities between groups; move one to a quieter group) after each generation. Most code, best fit for partition-shaped problems.

Lesson 12 · ~25 min · the payoff

The Exam Method — D·E·D·F·O

Everything from Lessons 1–11 compressed into the exact procedure you'll run on Friday, mapped mark-by-mark onto the scheme.

The pipeline: Decisions → Encoding → Decoder → Fitness → Others

  1. D — Decisions. List every choice the scenario leaves open. Check for the sneaky ones: on/off activation, order, timing. Anything decidable must appear in the chromosome.
  2. E — Encoding. Pick ONE pattern (default: assignment vector + permutation). Draw it with the scenario's real numbers. State legal values per gene. Two sentences vs a rejected alternative.
  3. D — Decoder. Numbered phases; why the order is forced.
  4. F — Fitness. Hard/soft table → constraint-tool table → components (formula + unit + purpose) → normalise → weights (cite the scenario) → Pareto alternative → critical awareness.
  5. O — Others. Q3: PSO or ACO under three headings, plus judgement sentence.

Sentence templates (adapt, don't recite)

  • “I assume that [X], because the brief does not state it. Without this assumption, [Y] would be impossible.”
  • “[Decision] is not given, so it must be encoded. I represent it by [gene / block position / row].”
  • “[Entity] may only use [resources], so I restrict that gene's legal values to [set] — the violation cannot be expressed and the search space shrinks.”
  • “The chromosome has [N] genes: genes [a–b] represent [what]; genes [c–d] represent [what].”
  • “The decoder first builds [phase 1], then [phase 2], because [2] cannot be determined until [1] is known.”
  • “A solution is valid if and only if it passes the following checks: …”
  • “These objectives conflict — improving [fᵢ] worsens [fⱼ] — so the weights express policy, not fact.”

The nine mistakes that cost marks (his list, generalised)

  1. Missing a decision — “the commonest and most expensive error.” Activation decisions especially.
  2. Encoding per-task what happens once — a ship docks once; 13 genes doing 4 genes' work is wasted search. Say so.
  3. Ignoring compatibility — a design that lets ship 2 use berth A is invalid from birth.
  4. No exclusivity rule — two entities on one resource at one time must be a hard check.
  5. Inventing complexity — if the scenario doesn't mention holidays, leave them out.
  6. Unstated assumptions — they carry explicit marks; write them first.
  7. A fitness that can't see what you care about — the makespan trap; show the critical-awareness paragraph.
  8. Adding raw units — normalise before combining.
  9. Q1 overrun — 40 minutes maximum; it's 15 of 40 marks.

What full marks looks like (calibrate your ambition)

He is “allergic to giving full marks” — the way to fight that is coverage of the scheme, not brilliance: every mark row in the scheme gets its own visible paragraph or table. An examiner can only award what they can find. Use headings matching the questions; underline component names; draw the chromosome.

“The area where students lose marks is in taking up the information” — i.e., not consuming the scenario's own numbers and rules.
